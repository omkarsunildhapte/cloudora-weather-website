What the colours are actually measuring

A weather radar sends out a narrow pulse of microwave energy and listens for the fraction that bounces back off raindrops, snowflakes and hailstones. The delay tells it how far away the target is; the strength of the returned signal tells it how much is up there. That strength is called reflectivity, and because it spans an enormous range it is reported on a logarithmic decibel scale: dBZ.

Reflectivity is not rainfall. It responds to the size of the drops far more strongly than to their number — a handful of large drops returns much more energy than a fog of tiny ones carrying the same amount of water. Converting dBZ to a rain rate therefore needs an assumption about drop-size distribution, and the standard relationships used for that are approximations tuned for typical rain. In winter the same echo can mean very different things on the ground, because snow and sleet return energy differently from rain and a radar cannot see the freezing level. That is why precipitation-type shading is usually a model product layered on top of the radar rather than something the radar itself measured.

Motion is the whole point — read the loop, not the frame

A single frame tells you where rain is. Two or three frames tell you where it is going, and that is the question you actually have. Scrub back through the loop, pick one distinct cell, and note how far it travels between frames. Extend that same displacement forward past your own location and you have a usable arrival estimate.

A few patterns are worth recognising while you do it:

  • Growing versus decaying. A cell whose core is brightening and expanding will likely still be raining when it reaches you. One that is fading between frames may dissolve before it arrives. Showers in the late afternoon often intensify; the same showers after sunset often collapse as the heating that fed them disappears.
  • Lines that move along themselves. When individual cells travel roughly parallel to the line they form, each one passes over the same ground in turn. Forecasters call this training, and it is the classic setup for flash flooding — a modest shower repeated fifteen times over one catchment.
  • Bowed segments. A line of storms that pushes outward into a bow shape is being shoved by a strong downdraught behind it. Bow echoes are associated with damaging straight-line winds, and are worth taking seriously even without a warning attached.
  • Gaps that aren't gaps. A hole in a band of rain moving towards you may simply close before it arrives. Check whether the gap has been widening or narrowing over the last few frames rather than assuming it will hold.

Extrapolating radar forward like this is called nowcasting, and it works well for about the next one to two hours. Beyond that, storms grow, die and change direction faster than a straight-line projection can follow, and a proper forecast model beats it.

When radar lies to you

Radar has well-known blind spots, and knowing them is most of what separates a confident reading from a wrong one.

The beam climbs. A radar beam leaves the dish at a slight upward tilt and the Earth curves away beneath it, so the further from the radar you look, the higher in the atmosphere it is sampling. Far from the site it may be looking straight over the top of shallow rain and drizzle entirely — the map says dry, the street says otherwise. The beam also spreads with distance, so distant features are smeared and small intense cores are under-reported.

Rain does not always land. An echo aloft falling into dry air can evaporate completely before reaching the ground. That is virga, and it is a common reason radar shows light returns over an area that stays entirely dry — especially in hot, dry climates and early in a storm's life.

Melting snow exaggerates. Just below the freezing level, snowflakes acquire a film of liquid water while keeping their large snowflake size. Big and wet is the ideal radar target, so a bright horizontal band of enhanced echo appears at that altitude. On distant scans that bright band can make ordinary rain look far heavier than it is.

Not everything the radar sees is weather. Buildings and hills near the site produce permanent clutter. Flocks of birds, insect swarms and even wind-turbine blades return signals. On calm nights an unusual temperature profile can bend the beam down into the ground, painting broad false echoes across the map — anomalous propagation. Modern dual-polarisation radars filter most of this out, but not all of it.

Using it in the app

Cloudora Weather's radar screen overlays live precipitation tiles on a map with a timeline scrubber, so stepping through the recent frames — the single most useful habit in this guide — takes a couple of seconds. Read the loop first, then check the hourly forecast for the rain probability in the slot you care about; the radar tells you what is happening now, and the forecast tells you what the atmosphere intends to do next. The two disagreeing is usually a sign of a shower that has yet to form.

If a band of rain is about to clear through, it is also worth glancing at air quality afterwards: rainfall scavenges particles out of the air, and the hours after a downpour are often the cleanest of the week.
