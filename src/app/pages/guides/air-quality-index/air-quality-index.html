An index is a translation, not a measurement

Air quality instruments don't measure "air quality". They measure concentrations: how many micrograms of one specific pollutant sit in a cubic metre of air, written µg/m³. Those numbers are exact and almost useless at a glance — is 38 µg/m³ of fine particulate a problem? An air quality index does the translation for you. It compares each measured pollutant against a set of health-based thresholds, converts each into a band, and reports the band of whichever pollutant is doing worst.

The consequence is that there is no single AQI. The United States EPA runs a 0–500 scale. The European Environment Agency publishes a six-band index. India, China and many other countries maintain their own. The same air genuinely can be "Moderate" on one scale and "Unhealthy for Sensitive Groups" on another, because the scales encode different regulatory thresholds — not different air. So a rating is only meaningful once you know which scale produced it.

The four pollutants that usually decide the number

PM2.5 is fine particulate matter: airborne particles 2.5 micrometres across or smaller, roughly a thirtieth the width of a human hair. That size matters. Particles this small slip past the nose and throat's defences, reach the alveoli deep in the lungs, and in the finest fractions cross into the bloodstream. It comes overwhelmingly from combustion — vehicle exhaust, coal and biomass burning, wildfire smoke — plus secondary particles that form in the air out of other pollutant gases. It carries the strongest evidence of long-term harm of any common pollutant, which is why it is usually the one setting the index.

PM10 is the coarser fraction, up to 10 micrometres: road dust, construction and demolition, agricultural soil, sea salt, pollen. It deposits higher up in the airway, so it irritates more than it penetrates. Because PM10 includes PM2.5 by definition, a PM10 spike with no matching PM2.5 rise almost always means dust rather than smoke.

Ozone at ground level isn't emitted by anything. It is manufactured in the air when sunlight drives reactions between nitrogen oxides and volatile organic compounds. That makes it a hot, bright, still-afternoon pollutant, typically peaking in the mid to late afternoon — and it can be worse in the suburbs downwind of a city than in the city centre, because the chemistry needs time and sunlight to run. It is a powerful airway irritant even in people with no respiratory condition.

Nitrogen dioxide comes mainly from combustion engines, diesel above all. It is the most local of the four: concentrations fall off sharply within roughly a hundred metres of a busy road, which is a large part of why the reading for your city can feel wrong for your street.

For context on how demanding "good" really is: the World Health Organization's 2021 air quality guidelines set an annual mean of 5 µg/m³ for PM2.5 and a 24-hour mean of 15 µg/m³, with 15 and 45 µg/m³ respectively for PM10. Those are health targets rather than expectations — most of the world's cities sit above them, and an index reading of "Good" does not automatically mean the air meets them.

What each level should change about your day

"Sensitive groups" is doing a lot of work in air quality advice. It means people with asthma, COPD or other chronic lung disease, people with heart disease, young children, older adults, and people who are pregnant. If that's you, act one band earlier than everyone else.

Why the reading moves so much

Most of the day-to-day variation is weather, not emissions. Wind disperses pollution and calm air lets it accumulate. A temperature inversion — a layer of warm air sitting on top of cold air near the ground — puts a lid on the lowest few hundred metres of the atmosphere and traps everything underneath it, which is why the worst readings in many cities come on still, cold winter mornings rather than on busy summer days. Rain scrubs particles out of the air, and the hours after a downpour are often the cleanest of the week.

Time of day matters too, and differently per pollutant: nitrogen dioxide tracks rush hour, ozone peaks with the afternoon sun, and particulate tends to rise in the evening where wood or coal is burned for heat. And every reading comes from a monitoring station that may be several kilometres away, so treat it as a good estimate for your neighbourhood rather than a measurement at your front door.
