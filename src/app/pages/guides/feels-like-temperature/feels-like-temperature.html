Your body doesn't measure air temperature

A thermometer reports one thing: the temperature of the air around it. What you experience is something else entirely — the rate at which your body is losing or gaining heat. You are a roughly 37 °C object continuously producing heat, and comfort is the state in which you are shedding that heat at exactly the rate you make it. Anything that changes the shedding rate changes how the same air feels.

Four channels do the shedding: conduction into things you touch, convection into moving air, radiation to and from your surroundings, and evaporation of sweat. Air temperature only sets the gradient for two of them. Humidity governs evaporation, wind governs convection, and sunlight adds radiation the shade never sees. A "feels like" figure — formally an apparent temperature — is an attempt to answer a single question: what still-air, dry, shaded temperature would give you the same heat balance as the conditions you are actually standing in?

Which is why there isn't one formula. In the heat, humidity dominates and you get a heat index. In the cold, wind dominates and you get wind chill. They are separate models with separate valid ranges, and the number your app shows is whichever one the conditions call for.

Warm and humid: why sweat stops working

Once air temperature approaches skin temperature, convection and radiation stop helping much — there's barely a gradient left to shed heat down. Evaporation becomes almost the whole cooling system: sweat absorbs a large amount of heat as it changes from liquid to vapour, and carries it away.

Evaporation is driven by the difference between the water vapour pressure at your wet skin and the vapour pressure of the surrounding air. When the air is already close to saturated, that difference is small, evaporation slows, and sweat simply runs off you doing no useful work at all. You are producing the same heat with a throttled cooling system, so your core temperature drifts up — and that is what "feels hotter" is actually describing.

Relative humidity is a poor guide to this, because it is relative: 70 per cent at 12 °C and 70 per cent at 32 °C describe wildly different amounts of water. Dew point — the temperature to which the air must cool for saturation — is an absolute measure, and it maps far more cleanly onto comfort.

The formal heat index used by national weather services is a regression fit to a much more complex human heat-balance model, and it is only defined over part of the range: broadly from about 27 °C upwards, and at relative humidity above roughly 40 per cent. Below that it has nothing to add, which is why a heat-index-based "feels like" often equals the air temperature on mild days rather than being missing. It also assumes shade and a light breeze. Standing in direct sun can add several degrees to the effective load, which is why occupational heat standards use wet-bulb globe temperature — a measure that includes radiant heat — rather than the heat index.

Cold and windy: why wind chill bites

In the cold, the mechanism inverts. Your body warms a thin boundary layer of air right against the skin, and that stagnant warm film is genuinely insulating — a good part of what clothing does is simply hold it in place. Wind strips it away and replaces it with fresh cold air, over and over, so heat leaves your skin far faster than the air temperature alone would suggest.

The wind chill formulas in use today were derived from a model of heat loss from an exposed adult face walking into the wind, with the wind speed measured at face height rather than at the standard ten-metre anemometer height. They apply at low temperatures — broadly at or below about 10 °C — and above a light wind threshold of a few kilometres per hour; outside that range the concept simply isn't defined.

Two things follow that are worth internalising. First, wind chill only describes exposed skin. It is not a temperature the world reaches: a parked car, a water pipe or a thermometer will not cool below the true air temperature no matter how hard the wind blows, because none of them is generating heat. Wind speeds up the approach to air temperature; it does not lower it. Second, wind chill is the number to take seriously for frostbite risk. On the standard charts, exposed skin can begin to freeze in around thirty minutes at a wind chill near −28 °C, and in roughly ten minutes near −35 °C.

Wind chill also assumes dry skin. Wet skin — rain, sweat, a soaking from a puddle — loses heat dramatically faster, because evaporation is now working against you. That combination, a few degrees above freezing with wind and rain, is the classic hypothermia setup, and it is one the wind chill number alone will under-state.

Reading the gap between the two numbers

The useful habit is to read the gap, not just the second number. A large positive gap — feels-like well above the actual reading — means humidity is high and your evaporative cooling is compromised: drink earlier than you think you need to, plan hard effort for the cooler ends of the day, and pay attention to how you feel rather than to the clock. A large negative gap means wind, and wind is a clothing problem: a windproof outer layer solves more of it than another fleece, because the fleece's job is holding still air that the wind is taking away.

When the two numbers are equal, that is information too. It means conditions are within the band where humidity and wind are not doing anything interesting, and the plain temperature is an honest guide to your day.

How the app shows it

Cloudora Weather displays the feels-like reading directly beneath the current temperature, so the gap between them is visible at a glance instead of being something you have to go looking for. The value comes from OpenWeatherMap's own apparent-temperature calculation, which blends air temperature, humidity and wind into a single figure — so it hands you the right model for the conditions rather than making you pick. Humidity and wind speed are both in the details strip if you want to see what is driving the difference.
