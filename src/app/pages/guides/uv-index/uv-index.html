What the number is measuring

The UV index is a measure of the strength of ultraviolet radiation reaching the ground, weighted by how much damage each wavelength does to human skin. That weighting is the part people miss. A UV meter doesn't just add up ultraviolet energy; it multiplies each wavelength by an internationally agreed "erythemal action spectrum" — a curve describing how efficiently that wavelength causes sunburn — before summing. One unit of UV index corresponds to 25 milliwatts per square metre of that damage-weighted irradiance.

This is why the index behaves so unlike the other numbers on a weather screen. It is not derived from temperature, it is not the same as how bright the sky looks, and it is not capped at 10 — readings above 11 are routine in the tropics and at altitude. The scale is open-ended by design.

Two bands of ultraviolet reach the surface and both matter. UVB (roughly 280–315 nanometres) is the sunburn band: it is absorbed in the outer skin, it causes the reddening the index is calibrated against, and it drives most direct DNA damage. UVA (315–400 nm) is weaker per photon but far more abundant, penetrates deeper into the dermis, drives photo-ageing, and contributes to skin cancer risk as well. UVA also passes through ordinary window glass, which UVB largely does not. A sunscreen labelled "broad spectrum" is one that covers both; an SPF number on its own only tells you about the UVB half.

Why UV and warmth come apart

Ultraviolet intensity is set almost entirely by how high the sun sits in the sky. The lower the sun, the longer the slanted path each ray takes through the atmosphere, and the more UV is absorbed before it arrives. That geometry is why UV peaks within an hour or two of solar noon — broadly the late-morning-to-mid-afternoon window — and why it collapses by early evening even on days that stay hot.

Four other things move the number, and none of them is temperature:

  • Altitude. There is less atmosphere overhead, so UV rises by roughly ten per cent for every thousand metres of elevation. Mountains combine this with cold air, which is exactly why alpine skiing produces so many surprise burns.
  • Reflection. Fresh snow can bounce back most of the UV that hits it, effectively doubling your exposure and delivering it from below, where hats do not help. Dry sand and open water reflect far less but still add meaningfully to a long day out.
  • Ozone. The stratospheric ozone column absorbs UVB before it reaches you, and it varies with season and latitude — part of why identical sun angles give different index values in different parts of the world.
  • Cloud. Thin or broken cloud blocks much less UV than it blocks visible light and warmth, so an overcast-looking day can still deliver a high index. Scattered cumulus can even push readings briefly above the clear-sky value, as light reflects off the bright edges of clouds and adds to the direct beam.

What sunscreen actually does

SPF describes how much longer skin protected by a correctly applied film takes to redden compared with unprotected skin, and it maps to a filtering fraction that flattens out fast: SPF 15 absorbs roughly 93 per cent of UVB, SPF 30 about 97 per cent, and SPF 50 about 98 per cent. The jump from 15 to 30 is worth making; the jump from 30 to 50 buys you a little margin for imperfect application rather than a step change in protection.

The catch is "correctly applied". SPF is measured in the laboratory at 2 milligrams of product per square centimetre of skin — about a teaspoon for the face and neck, and the better part of a shot glass for a whole adult body in swimwear. Most people apply somewhere between a quarter and a half of that, and protection falls off faster than the shortfall suggests. Applying the labelled SPF thinly is closer to wearing a much lower SPF than to wearing the one on the bottle.

Reapply every two hours while you are out, and immediately after swimming, heavy sweating or towelling off, regardless of what the bottle claims about water resistance. And treat sunscreen as the last line rather than the first: shade in the middle of the day, a close-woven shirt, a brimmed hat and wrap-around sunglasses all outperform it, because none of them wears off.
